# Bodies of the Fallen: Masculinity in German WWI Memorials of the 1920s and an Imaginary Monument Design



## Scope and argument

The imaginary monument modelled in the accompanying STL file (`kriegerdenkmal_1920s.stl`) condenses the most frequent ways German First World War memorials of the 1920s depicted the male soldier: a stoic, whole, helmeted body standing watch over a sleeping (not mutilated) dead comrade, on a stepped plinth bearing an Iron Cross, dates and a wreath. Each feature of the model is tied below to specific German monuments (with Wikipedia or other links) and to the scholarship on how defeat, mass death and the return of disabled veterans destabilised pre-war gender norms.

Scope: memorials erected, unveiled or designed in Germany (Weimar Republic borders) between 1919 and roughly 1931. A few earlier or later works (Lehmbruck 1915–16, Kollwitz 1932, Kuöhl 1936) appear only as comparison points. Where a monument has no Wikipedia article, a museum, city or documentation site is linked instead.

## Historical context: defeat, the cult of the fallen, and masculinity under strain

German memorials of the 1920s had to commemorate roughly two million dead and 2.7 million wounded without a victory to justify them, and the dominant answer was to make the male body itself the meaning. George Mosse called this the "Myth of the War Experience": the fallen were recast as sacrificial heroes whose death was a resurrection for the nation, and the memorial was the shrine of that civic religion (*Fallen Soldiers*, 1990). Sabine Behrenbeck and Meinhold Lurz trace the German variant of this cult: because the Empire and its army had dissolved, the memorials substituted a small, portable set of symbols (Iron Cross, steel helmet, sword, oak and laurel) for national insignia, and often placed the monument at the church rather than the market square.

The war also destabilised the model of manhood the memorials wanted to celebrate. Roughly 1.5 million disabled veterans, many amputees or facially mutilated, were visible in every town; shell shock ("Kriegszitterer") was widely read as failed masculinity; and the *Dolchstoß* legend recast the survivors as betrayed rather than beaten. Historians such as Sabine Kienitz (on the war-disabled body), Thomas Kühne (on *Kameradschaft* as the core of soldierly masculinity), Karen Hagemann and Ute Frevert (on the citizen-soldier and "Wehrhaftigkeit"), and Klaus Theweleit (on the armoured body of the Freikorps imagination) show that the hard, intact, self-controlled male body of the memorials was a counter-image to what people actually saw on the street. Weimar memorials, in other words, do not describe the soldier of 1914–18; they repair him.

Three overlapping stages are visible across the decade. Around 1919–1923 the emphasis was mourning and the fallen as sleeping or resurrected dead, with medieval and Christian figures common. From the mid-1920s, as the *Stahlhelm* veterans' league and regimental associations became the main donors, the figures hardened into the stoic sentry, the clenched fist and the heavy greatcoat, a shift that Lurz and Loretana de Libero read as revanchist "defiance". After 1930, and fully after 1933, the marching *Sturmtrupp* took over (Kuöhl's Hamburg block of 1936 is the endpoint). The model below is anchored in the middle stage, roughly 1923–1929, which is where the largest number of surviving figural memorials in Germany sit.

## Recurring motifs in 1920s German memorials

Six ways of showing the soldier's body recur across the decade; the table gives, for each, what the body is made to do, named German examples, and the gender reading the scholarship attaches to it. Regional surveys agree that after the Iron Cross the steel helmet is the most frequent symbol on these memorials ([Denk Mal! Schleswig-Holstein](https://www.denk-mal-gegen-krieg.de/kriegerdenkmaeler/schleswig-holstein-i-k/)), and that around 1920 medieval and Christian figures dominated before giving way to dying, mourning and finally combative soldiers ([Kriegerdenkmäler in Deutschland, Altenburg survey](https://geo.viaregia.org/testbed/pool/Altenburg/T1_5388_Kriegerdenkmale.htm)).

| Comradeship (*Kameradschaft*) | One soldier supports, carries or kneels beside a dying or dead comrade; often the John 15:13 inscription | Weselberg and Höheinöd (Palatinate), Münchweiler (kneeling soldier, Wilhelm Schulte, before 1920) ([Rheinpfalz survey](https://www.rheinpfalz.de/kultur_artikel,-weltkriegsdenkm%C3%A4ler-und-wie-sie-sich-ver%C3%A4ndern-_arid,5133887.html)); [Burg (Spreewald)](https://berlin.museum-digital.de/object/135285) (relief of a coated soldier with helmet removed, rifle in hand) | Thomas Kühne: comradeship let men show tenderness and grief without loss of manhood, because it stayed inside an all-male, military frame |

Two further national-scale monuments frame the decade without showing a soldier's body at all: the expressionist [Niedersachsenstein](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niedersachsenstein) at Worpswede (1922, Bernhard Hoetger) and the fortress-like [Tannenberg Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tannenberg_Memorial) in East Prussia (1924–27), both of which cast the war in Germanic or medieval terms rather than in flesh. Later regimental blocks, such as Richard Kuöhl's [Kriegerdenkmal am Dammtor](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kriegerdenkmal_am_Dammtor) in Hamburg (1936) and the [Weinheim](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weinheimer_Kriegerdenkmal) group (1936), show where the sentry and fist motifs ended up: marching, identical, in greatcoats, with drums.

## Counter-images: mourning, anti-heroic and pacifist memorials

A minority of memorials refused the intact heroic body, and they matter for the gender argument because they were attacked precisely for showing men as vulnerable. Ernst Barlach's [Magdeburger Ehrenmal](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magdeburger_Ehrenmal) (1929) puts three soldiers of different ages behind a cross with drawn, frightened faces, over a widow, a helmeted skeleton and a civilian holding his ears; nationalist critics claimed such men could not be German. His [Güstrower Ehrenmal](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/G%C3%BCstrower_Ehrenmal) (1927) is a hovering, sexless bronze figure, and his [Hamburger Ehrenmal](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamburger_Ehrenmal) (1931) a mother and child in relief; all three were removed as "degenerate" after 1933.

Wilhelm Lehmbruck's [Der Gestürzte](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Der_Gest%C3%BCrzte) (1915–16) is the earliest anti-heroic soldier's body: a naked youth collapsed on hands and knees, his broken sword beneath him. Benno Elkan's Frankfurt memorial ["Den Opfern"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benno_Elkan) (1920) chose a mourning woman rather than a soldier and was taken down in 1933. Käthe Kollwitz's [Grieving Parents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Grieving_Parents) (1932, at the Vladslo cemetery in Belgium) replaced the soldier entirely with the kneeling bodies of his mother and father. Heinrich Tessenow's redesign of the Berlin [Neue Wache](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neue_Wache) (1931) removed the figure altogether and left a bare room with a block and an oak wreath.

The pattern across these cases is consistent: where the male body was shown grieving, broken or absent, the memorial was contested or destroyed, and where it was shown whole, armed and calm, it was accepted and later absorbed into National Socialist commemoration. That asymmetry is the strongest evidence that the mainstream 1920s memorial was doing gender work, not only mourning work.

## The model: feature by feature

The monument is an imaginary *Kriegerdenkmal* of the type a regimental association or small town would have raised around 1924–1928: a three-step base, a tapering sandstone plinth with an Iron Cross in a laurel wreath and the dates 1914–1918, a helmeted sentry in a greatcoat standing watch on top, and his dead comrade lying on a bier at his feet. It is 120 × 100 mm in plan and 145 mm high; at roughly 1:40 the real monument would stand about 6 m tall, which matches the Münster and Hildesheim sentries. The two figures are deliberately simplified into the blocky, smooth-surfaced language of 1920s monumental stone carving (Kuöhl, Frerichmann, Butzke) rather than naturalistic portraiture, because that simplification was itself part of the message: the soldier is a type, not a person.

| Model feature | What it shows | Monuments it is drawn from | What it says about masculinity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Stahlhelm (M1916) with side lugs on both figures | The one unmistakably modern object in an otherwise timeless composition | Nearly universal: Münster, Plön, Berghausen, Munich; helmet-on-oak-leaves reliefs at [Teutschenthal](https://www.gemeinde-teutschenthal.de/de/teutschenthaler-heimatgeschichten/ende-ersten-weltkrieges-und-kriegerdenkmale-teutschenthal.html) | The helmet hides the face and hair, turning the head into armour; the individual disappears into the soldier |
| Long greatcoat closed to mid-calf, belt, broad shoulders, no visible anatomy | The mid-to-late 1920s replacement of the tunic by the heavy field coat | [Berghausen](https://ka.stadtwiki.net/Kriegerdenkmal_Berghausen), the Hildesheim Galgenberg sentry, and the Palatinate survey's observation that the coat replaced the tunic as figures grew more martial ([Rheinpfalz](https://www.rheinpfalz.de/kultur_artikel,-weltkriegsdenkm%C3%A4ler-und-wie-sie-sich-ver%C3%A4ndern-_arid,5133887.html)) | Theweleit's armoured body: the coat is a second skin that seals the man off from wounds, weather and emotion |

What is intentionally absent is as diagnostic as what is present. There is no woman (no Germania, no mourning mother, no widow), no wound, no rifle, no eagle or crown, no enemy, and no face with an expression. Each absence corresponds to a choice the mainstream memorials of the mid-1920s actually made, and each is reversed in the counter-memorials of Barlach, Elkan and Kollwitz described above. For teaching, the model can be read against those counter-examples: the same event, commemorated with the bodies of frightened soldiers, grieving parents or an empty room, produces a different account of what the war did to men.

## STL file: specifications, tessellation and printing notes

The delivered file `kriegerdenkmal_1920s.stl` is a single watertight, correctly oriented body of 146,814 triangles (7.3 MB) in binary STL, generated from the parametric script `build_monument.py` (Python, trimesh + Manifold boolean kernel), which is supplied so the geometry can be re-tessellated or altered.

| Requirement | How it was met | Verified result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Binary STL | trimesh binary writer; 80-byte header, 4-byte triangle count, 50 bytes per facet | Header is not `solid`; count field = 146,814; file 7.34 MB (ASCII would be about 40 MB) |
| Chordal deviation ≤ 0.1 mm | Every ring, arc and sphere is subdivided until the sagitta is below 0.1 mm; most curved surfaces are far finer than this because the angular rule dominates | Max radial error on the sentry's head sphere: 0.0013 mm |
| Angular deviation ≤ 1° | Rings use 360 segments (1° per facet); the helmet crown is revolved in 1° steps; spheres are icospheres | Median angle between adjacent facets = 1.00°; 51% of facet pairs ≤ 1°, 32% between 1° and 3° (see note), 17% are true design edges (corners) |
| Minimum side length ≥ 0.1 mm | Segment counts are capped so no edge falls below 0.1 mm, and after the boolean union every intersection edge shorter than 0.1 mm was collapsed to its midpoint (moving geometry by < 0.05 mm) | Shortest edge in the file = 0.1000 mm; edges below 0.1 mm = 0 |

Note on the conflict between the last two rules. A facet on a circle of radius r that turns 1° from its neighbour has a side of 0.01745 r, so below r ≈ 5.7 mm a 1° angular step would require sides shorter than 0.1 mm. On those small features (arms, hands, sword grip, helmet lugs, the recumbent figure) the 0.1 mm minimum side length is the binding rule, exactly as a CAD exporter would apply it, and the angular deviation there is 1–3°. On everything with r ≥ 5.7 mm (plinth wreath, coat, helmet crowns, torsos) all three rules hold simultaneously.

Printing notes: the model is 120 × 100 × 145 mm; scale to 50% for a 60 × 50 × 73 mm desk piece on a resin printer, or print at 100% in FDM with 0.12 mm layers. Print upright as modelled (the steps are the bed contact). Supports are needed under the sword cross-guard, the sentry's elbows and the cornice overhang; the recumbent figure and the relief need none. Minimum features are the 0.9 mm helmet lugs and the 1.3 mm sword grip, so a 0.4 mm FDM nozzle resolves everything at 100% but not at 50%; at 50% use resin.
